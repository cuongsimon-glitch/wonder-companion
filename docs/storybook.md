# Animated storybook design for Wonder Companion

Design basis for turning the adventure into a picture book: Pip answers the child's question through about three lessons inside a story that ends in triumph. Numbers in square brackets point to section 8.

**Access note.** WebFetch could not open any page in this session (every host failed to resolve), so I read no paper in full. Every claim comes from WebSearch result summaries. Check effect sizes in the papers before quoting them.

## 1. Summary

1. **Move the pictures, skip the extras.** Animated pictures, music and sound effects helped comprehension and vocabulary in a 43-study meta-analysis. Hotspots, games and dictionaries tended to hurt, because the child switches tasks [1][2]. A 2026 meta-analysis found interactive features averaged near zero [3]. No games and no tap-to-explore pictures.
2. **Match each picture to its sentence.** Children look longer at details the text names [4].
3. **Facts realistic, Pip fantastical.** Anthropomorphic language with unrealistic pictures hurt preschoolers' fact learning; factual language with realistic pictures did not seem to [6]. Children transfer more from realistic stories [8][9].
4. **Highlight words as they are read aloud, and offer a read-alone mode.** Whole-passage highlighting beat gaze-triggered highlighting in one project [18]. Same-language subtitling shows promise for reading, but the evidence is mostly the programme's own [14][15].
5. **Captions are a reading aid, not a learning aid.** Narration plus identical text hurt adults' retention [12]. Keep captions optional.
6. **Give each lesson three exposures:** text, picture, recap.
7. **Try, fail, try, fail, succeed.** Craft convention, not tested science [storytelling.md]. It matches the fall-then-rise "man in a hole" arc [26].
8. **Big, short text in a clear sans-serif.** Smaller type and longer lines lowered second graders' comprehension [22].

## 2. What the evidence says about animated storybooks

| Feature | Effect | Evidence | What we do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Animated pictures | Helped | 43 studies, 2,147 children; small overall technology gain: g = 0.17 comprehension, 0.20 expressive vocabulary [1] | One gentle motion per page that shows the sentence. |
| Music, sound effects | Helped | Same meta-analysis [1]. Sound may disrupt speech perception for children with verbal-processing difficulty [4] | Quiet music, few sounds, mute option. |
| Hotspots | Hurt or neutral | Even story-related ones did not reliably help; task switching suggested [1]. Near-zero average in 2026 [3] | None on pictures. |
| Games in the story | Hurt | Divert focus from the story [1][4] | None. |
| Dictionary | Mixed | Word-learning gains in some e-book studies [20]; tended to hurt comprehension in the meta-analysis [1] | Glossary on the recap page only. |
| Picture-text congruence | Helped attention | Children fixate longer on illustration details the text names [4] | Rule 7d: picture shows what the words say. |
| Home background | Multimedia helps and interactivity hurts more for less stimulating homes | [1] | Another reason to stay simple. |
| Computer versus adult | Multimedia stories beat print stories with no adult (g = 0.40); no claim they match an adult [4] | Takacs 2014 [4] | Invite adult reading; add dialogic prompts. |

Smeets and Bus (2015) tested animation plus hotspots for kindergartners' word learning. I saw the question, not the result [5].

**Mayer's principles, for a narrated picture book.** Coherence, signalling, redundancy and the two contiguity principles reduce extraneous load; modality is in another group [10]. Reported medians: signalling 0.41, spatial contiguity 0.82, temporal 1.31 [10]. I found no test with young children, so these are defaults.

- **Coherence:** cut decoration not in the sentence.
- **Signalling:** a lantern glow on the key object.
- **Spatial contiguity:** text inside or beside the picture.
- **Temporal contiguity:** animate as the sentence is read.
- **Modality:** narration with pictures beats text alone.
- **Redundancy:** identical narration and text hurt adults [12][13]. Yet children learning to read need the text, and one school-age study went the other way [13]. So captions are optional.

## 3. Aided captions and reading support

**Evidence.**
- **Same-language subtitling.** The 2007 IIM Ahmedabad study revisited over 13,000 people across five states; it concluded subtitling contributed significantly to reading improvement [14]. Kothari said three to five years of weekly exposure moved weak readers to functional [16]. A survey claim of doubled fifth-grade functional readers is from a news article only [16]. I found no independent replication [15]. Reviews of captioned TV support word-reading gains in school-age children [17].
- **Highlighting.** UCL eye-tracking: highlighting new words for the whole reading gave the biggest vocabulary gains; gaze-triggered highlighting did not help [18]. Samples are small.
- **Tap to hear a word.** I found no study of it.
- **Dialogic reading.** PEER (Prompt, Evaluate, Expand, Repeat) and CROWD prompts (Completion, Recall, Open-ended, Wh-questions, Distancing) [21]. Five studies showed significant oral-language effects [21], but one meta-analysis credited it with about 4% of vocabulary growth, and a 2020 trial beat no active control [21]. NAEYC notes children learned most from e-books with an adult helping [19].

**Build in this order.**
1. **Read-aloud with highlighting** of the spoken word, text always visible.
2. **Read-it-myself mode:** no voice, no highlight, pictures still animate.
3. **Voice speed and replay-sentence.**
4. **Tap a word in the text to hear it** (text only, untested, low risk).
5. **Page-end prompts** for adult or child to say aloud ("What will Pip try?"). No tapping, no scoring.
6. **Re-read mode** for the tablet: prompts off, recap one tap away.

## 4. Teaching real facts through a fantasy hero

**Risk.** Ganea et al. (2014) gave preschoolers books on obscure animals with realistic pictures and factual or anthropomorphic text; anthropomorphic books affected younger children's fact learning and their attribution of human traits [6]. Coverage said factual text with realistic pictures did not seem to hurt [6]. The 2018 review adds: the more a picture resembles the real thing, the easier transfer is [8]. Richert et al. (2009) found 3.5 to 5 year olds transferred solutions from real characters more than fantasy ones [9]. Fantasy can also help: one study found fantastical themes boosted vocabulary, and surprise may prompt a search for explanations [8]. Hybrid books may blend facts with plausible wrong detail; I could not trace that to one paper [28].

**Brief correction.** I found no Strouse, Nesbitt & Troseth (2018). The paper found is Strouse, Nyhout & Ganea (2018) [8].

**Our rules.**
1. Pip and named fantasy helpers may talk. Real animals and objects never do.
2. Real things are drawn realistically: true proportion, no clothes, no human faces.
3. Facts come in the narrator's voice, in plain realistic terms.
4. The villain may mislead but never states a false fact as true. Pip corrects any claim with evidence on the same page.
5. Page 11 is a realistic diagram page with no fantasy.
6. Say the bridge once: "Pip is pretend. Foxes are real."
7. The teacher checks facts before the story is shown (already built).

## 5. Readable text

I found no study giving words per page by age. Industry norms are 400 to 800 words for picture books aged 3 to 7 and about 1,000 to 2,500 for early readers, and sources disagree [25]. Numbers below are my judgement; test on the real tablet.

| | Ages 4-6 | Ages 7-9 | Ages 10-11 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Words per page | 15-30 | 30-60 | 60-100 |
| Words in 12 pages | 250-350 | 400-650 | 700-1,100 |
| Sentence length | 5-8 words | 8-12 words | 10-16 words |
| Type size (tablet) | 30-34 px | 24-28 px | 20-24 px |
| Line length | 30-40 characters | 40-50 | 45-60 |
| Repetition | Refrain every page | Refrain on the three tries | Optional |

**Type evidence.** In 100 second and fifth graders, smaller type and longer lines lowered second graders' comprehension; fifth graders were not hurt and did slightly better with smaller type, read as a "desirable difficulty" [22]. Typeface choice mattered less: one search result said four typefaces were read equally well [24].

**Typeface.** Andika was designed for beginning readers, with hard-to-confuse letters [23]. Atkinson Hyperlegible was designed for low vision, not early reading [23]. Use Andika for story text, Atkinson Hyperlegible for buttons. I found no test of either with children.

**Repetition.** Repeat "Pip tried..." for each try. No controlled study found; craft convention.

## 6. How the earlier research is reused

| `storytelling.md` finding | In the game | In the book |
|---|---|---|
| Story Spine | Backbone of four chapters | Backbone of 12 pages; pages 5 to 8 are the "because of that" chain, each starting from the last result. |
| Kishotenketsu | Chapter 3 twist | Page 7 is the twist: a clue seen from a new angle. Keeps the villain cheeky, not scary. |
| Rule of three | Three clues, escalating obstacles | Three tries, three lessons, three exposures per fact. |
| Three Clue Rule | Child taps six clue kinds | No tapping. Each fact appears in text, picture and recap. The clue kinds (spot, listen, compare, ask, test, pattern) become what Pip does. |
| Curiosity gap | Each chapter opens a small question | Page 1 poses the child's question as Pip's. Each lesson page ends on a smaller question at the page turn. |
| Kind setbacks | Setback reveals a clue | Each "down" page is funny and carries a fact. Praise Pip's trying. |
| Consequences carry forward | Hearts, backpack, villain power | Pip's choice on page 4 or 5 visibly matters on page 6 or 9. No stored state, just a written chain. |
| Three endings | State picks one | One ending, triumph, as briefed. The muddle-through and second-try endings are dropped. |

The child no longer chooses; Pip does. Keep some agency through page-end talk prompts rather than tapping.

**Emotional arcs.** A text analysis of Gutenberg fiction (1,327 or 1,737 books; versions differ) found six core arcs, including "man in a hole" [26]. A claim that some arcs got more downloads is from a popular write-up I did not verify [26]. A 1983 report found young children preferred positive endings (old, not peer reviewed) [27]; a Scholastic guide says five-year-olds respond to triumph over adversity (advice, not research) [27]. Others argue happy endings are a modern habit [27]. Triumph is our brief, so it is a design choice.

## 7. Recommended book template

### 7a. Page by page

| Page | Spine | Fortune | Job and lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Once upon a time | Neutral | Meet Pip. The child's question becomes Pip's. |
| 2 | Every day | Neutral | Pip's world. Cheeky villain appears. Pip decides to find out. |
| 3 | Until one day | Up | **Try 1.** First idea. **L1** stated in text. |
| 4 | Because of that | Down | Try 1 fails funnily. Picture shows L1. Pip makes a choice. |
| 5 | Because of that | Up | **Try 2** from the choice. **L2**, found by a different clue kind. |
| 6 | Because of that | Down | Villain trick or Pip's earlier choice bites. L2 picture. |
| 7 | Because of that (twist) | **Lowest** | A clue seen a new way. A helper's question opens **L3**. Gentle. |
| 8 | Because of that | Rising | **Try 3.** L3 found. Notebook holds three lessons. |
| 9 | Until finally | **Triumph** | Pip uses L1, L2, L3 together. Villain's plan fails. |
| 10 | Ever since | Warm | Villain joins in or is outwitted kindly. Pip praised for trying. |
| 11 | (Real world) | Calm | **Plain answer.** Realistic diagram, narrator voice, one answer sentence. |
| 12 | Recap | Calm | **Recap.** Three lessons, three small pictures, three sentences, "read again". |

### 7b. Each lesson three ways

- **Text:** one plain narrator sentence on the discovery page.
- **Picture:** the same page shows the evidence realistically, with a lantern glow on it.
- **Recap:** page 12 repeats the sentence, shorter, beside a copy of the earlier picture. Page 11 adds the whole answer.

At least two exposures should be different in kind (words, picture, comparison).

### 7c. Rules for the AI prompt

1. Write exactly 12 pages following 7a, within the age band's word limits (section 5).
2. Answer the question through exactly three lessons, one fact each, taken only from the teacher-checked list.
3. Only Pip and named fantasy helpers speak or wear clothes. Real animals and objects never do.
4. State each fact once in a plain narrator sentence, with the same wording on page 12.
5. The villain never states a false fact as true. Correct misleading claims on the same page.
6. Pip tries three times: fail funny, fail funny, succeed. No failure is cruel, scary or humiliating.
7. A choice on page 4 or 5 must change something visible on page 6 or 9.
8. Page 7 is a gentle low point and twist. Page 9 is a clear triumph using all three lessons.
9. Praise effort and noticing, not being clever.
10. Page 11 is the plain answer: realistic, no fantasy, no jokes.
11. Page 12 recaps one short sentence per lesson, in the order found.
12. Give each page a one-line `scene` (see 7d) and an optional adult `prompt`. Never write hotspots, games or "tap to" lines.
13. Use short sentences and one refrain for the tries.
14. End on a warm, plain line with no open question.

### 7d. What each illustration must show

- **Every noun** in the page text is visible: Pip, lantern, fox, camera.
- **The verb** is the animation: "Pip lifts the lantern" is what moves.
- **The fact** on lesson pages: its evidence is clearly visible and realistically drawn, such as a correctly sized paw print.
- **The mood:** warm light on up pages, a dimmed lantern on down pages.
- **The villain's trick:** shown when the text says it, not before.
- **Real things realistic:** true proportion and colour; only Pip and helpers are fantastical.
- **Page 11:** a clean diagram with large labels.
- **Page 12:** three small pictures in lesson order, copies of the earlier ones.
- **No extras:** no decorative figures, hidden objects or motion unrelated to the sentence.
